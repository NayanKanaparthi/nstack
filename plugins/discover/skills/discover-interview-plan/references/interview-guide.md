# How to draft the interview plan

This is the Berkley Center interview approach, from Professor Cynthia Franklin's needs discovery materials for the Startup Lab, written in this kit's own words. The page credits it as "Berkley Center interview guide". Don't copy wording from her tip sheet or from any book; write fresh questions for this student's assumptions.

## The idea behind it

- You learn from what people did, not from what they say they might do. Ask about the past and the present, never the future.
- The person should know your general topic, not what you hope to hear.
- Listen far more than you talk. A good interview is mostly the other person speaking.
- Don't spend their time on anything a web search already answered. The web findings on the page come first; the interview goes where the web couldn't.
- The guide steers the conversation; it is not read out word for word. The student follows the person and comes back to it.

## Sections, in order

Each question is tagged to the assumption it tests, except warm-up and wrap-up questions, which may be untagged. Each question gets 1 to 3 short probes: follow-ups to use when the answer stays on the surface. A probe adds no time, place, reason, cost, payment or person the main question doesn't name: under "When did a sitter last fall through, if ever?", ask "If it happened: what did you do instead?", not "What did you do instead that evening?"; under "How did you choose the way you get roof measurements now?", ask "What did you weigh up?", not "What does it cost you each time?". A question never assumes the person has something they haven't mentioned (a tool, a crew, a team): "When did work last stop to get more materials, if ever?", not "When did your crew last stop ...?".

**"If ever" and "If yes".** A question asking when something last happened ends with ", if ever" ("When did you last chase a client for money, if ever?"). The only exception is a probe whose main question asks whether it happened. When a main question can be answered "no" or "never", a probe that fits only the other answer starts with it: "If yes:", "If so:" or "If it happened:" ("If yes: what did you do next?"). A generic follow-up to a story ("What happened next?") needs nothing.

**Tags.** Tag each question to the assumption its answer could show true or false for this person. A question about who had a say tests an assumption about who decides, not one about which tool they use.

1. **warm-up.** One or two easy questions about the person's role and situation, so they relax and talk freely. Example shape: "What does a normal week look like for you when it comes to <part of life>?"
2. **last time.** Get one specific, recent story. Example shapes: "Walk me through the most recent time <the problem> happened, if it has." "What happened just before that?" Probes: "What did you do next?", "Can you show me how you do that today?"
3. **how important.** Is this a small irritation they live with, or something they work hard to fix? Ask what they have already done about it, what else they tried, how they handle it now, and what they like most and least about how they handle it. Never ask what it would take to make them switch.
4. **how costly.** Put a size on the pain: time, money, people, stress. How often does it happen? When did it last happen? Ask for real amounts from the past ("How much time did that take you last month?"), never estimates of a future.
5. **how they chose.** How did they find out about what they use now? How did they decide? Who else had a say? Where did they get it?
6. **wrap-up.** Is there anything they want to add? Anything you should have asked and didn't? Who else should you talk to? Always ask for the next person.
7. **commitment.** Only at the very end, after the interview itself is done. One concrete ask that costs them a little: a follow-up conversation, an introduction to someone, a sign-up for a pilot. Ask it directly ("Can I ...?", "Could you introduce me to ...?"). Don't describe the product in detail; if they ask what you're working on, keep it to one plain sentence and go back to listening.

**Dig for the root cause.** When an answer explains a problem, ask "why" again, gently, a few times, until you reach something that isn't a symptom. A student who says "I stopped cooking at home" might, three whys later, be describing a shared kitchen with no storage, not a lack of time.

**Listen for:** makeshift fixes that treat a symptom; what the person values and what they don't; habits they follow without thinking; gaps between what they do and what they think they should do.

## Questions to avoid

The separate question check flags these, and the page refuses the first two kinds:

- **future:** what they will or would do ("Would you use ...?", "Will you ...?").
- **hypothetical:** imagined situations ("If there was an app ...", "Imagine you could ...", "If you could change one thing ...").
- **yes/no:** answerable with one word ("Do you find this annoying?"). Ask open questions instead.
- **leading:** points to the answer you hope for ("Isn't it frustrating when ...?", "Would you agree that ...").
- **pitch:** describes the student's idea or product, or names a solution or technology before the person has brought it up.
- **two questions:** two things in one sentence ("How often does it happen and what does it cost?").

Plain rule for drafting: never use "would", "will you", "you'd" or "if there was" in an interview question.

## Three big questions

The three things the student most needs to learn, each tagged to an assumption, usually the riskiest ones and the interview-only ones. At least one should feel uncomfortable to ask: one whose honest answer could show the problem isn't real for this person, or that asks what they have already spent. Example shapes: "When did this last cost you something, if ever?" "What have you tried and stopped using, if anything?"

## Money questions

Three questions, each about the past or present:

- what they spend on this today, in money or time;
- who holds the budget or makes the call to pay;
- what they tried and stopped paying for, and why.

## Pass marks

A pass mark counts past actions, so it never sits on a would-claim.

- For each riskiest assumption about the past or present (`problem`, `people`, `today`), write one pass mark on that assumption.
- For each riskiest `future` (would) assumption, put the pass mark on its `nearest_searchable` assumption, or another past-behaviour assumption, and list the would-claim in `stands_in_for`. If that assumption already has a pass mark, add the would-claim to its `stands_in_for`. Never attach a past-behaviour measure to a would-claim itself.
- `measure`: a short phrase, 15 words or fewer, naming something people did that the student can count from their notes: "Described paying for a stock tool in the past year". Every condition you name (a time, a place, a size) must be something an interview note can show, because a fact counts only when the notes show every condition.
- **A time condition names a window** ("in the past 3 months", "in the past year"), counted back from the interview's date, never "recent" alone. Take the window from the student's words (the kill criteria, the stress-test answers), or leave the time out.
- **A measure starts with a past action:** "Described ...", "Paid ...", "Tried ...", "Cancelled ...", "Switched ...". Never "Said", "Thinks" or "Feels": the page counts only what people did, so a measure about what they said can never move.
- **A how-often assumption** ("often", "most weeks", "at least twice a week") is measured by incidents the person describes in a stated window: "Described 2 or more wrong orders in the past month". The window is in the measure; the number of incidents comes from the student's own words, never a cutoff you invent.
- **A quality word** ("trusted", "reliable", "good") is measured by what the person did that shows it: "Turned down sitters they hadn't met", not "Had no trusted sitter".
- Keep the student's hedges. If their story says "maybe twice a week", don't write "at least twice a week" as if they had said it.
- Count actions, not opinions or promises.
- **The guide collects what the student's own tests need** (v1.7 point 9). Every cost, time window or amount that a pass mark's measure or the kill criteria depend on is asked in the guide, tagged to the assumption it belongs to, so the notes can show it. A question flagged only for its tag is fixed by re-tagging it, never by replacing it.
- Set `out_of` to 10 (the student can change it) and `mark` to `null`. The number is the student's: setting it before the interviews is the point, and a rough guess is fine. Never propose a number.

## Who to talk to

`talk_to` describes a kind of person, from `who_counts` and the riskiest assumptions: their role and situation ("roofers who quote jobs from measurement reports"). If the student named someone in the stress test, describe only the kind of person they are. Never a specific person's name, town, employer, crew or business size, or relation to the student.

## Where to find people

Places, never individuals:

- places where counted voices were found: the `where` and links of counted items (a forum, a review site's community, a subreddit);
- the student's own answer about who they can reach;
- at most 2 searches for communities or events where these people gather (clubs, associations, meetups, campus groups, online communities). Never open a site that mirrors, archives or scrapes another site's posts (a Reddit mirror, for example); link only the original community's own page, and if it won't open, don't list it.

Name each place as a place or a channel, never as a set of people: "Twitter/X direct messages to designers you follow", not "designers you follow on Twitter". Never list a person's name, email, phone number or profile. Spell out an organisation's short name the first time.

A place's `why` line states only what the student said about it, or what the place's own page visibly shows a reader ("Its page lists a trade show on 2 and 3 February 2027"). Never use a page's hidden description text, and never add a detail nobody stated ("you can reach in person"). If neither gives a reason, say what the place is, with no claim: "An online forum for roofers".

## Outreach messages

Two messages, for two different channels the student can use (from their answer: a campus group post, a LinkedIn message, an email to a club, a forum post). Each one:

- is short: four or five sentences, each under 25 words. A greeting on its own line ("Hi Sam,") runs on into the first sentence on the page's count, so keep that first sentence short too;
- says they are an NYU student;
- asks for their advice in so many words ("I'd value your advice"; you may add "and experience"), for 15 to 20 minutes. Asking only to hear about their experience is not enough;
- says they are not selling anything;
- offers to share what they learn;
- doesn't describe or pitch the idea;
- may use one short phrase from `evidence.language` so it sounds like the reader's own words.

Before saving, read each message once more. A message without the word "advice" is redrafted. The renderer refuses it too.

## Notes sheet

Keep the default fields: pain, workaround, money, goal, obstacle, people mentioned, follow-up. The page prints them with the big questions and the guide on one sheet.
