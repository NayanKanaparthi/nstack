# Step 1: Sharpen and sort

Turn what the student wrote into statements that could turn out to be wrong, and agree on them before any search. The test gets written first because once you have seen search results, it is very hard not to bend the hypothesis toward whatever you found.

## 1. Get the raw material

You need three things: the idea in a sentence, who it is for, and the student's assumptions. If one is missing, or too vague to search for (for example "equipment" without saying which), ask. Put all your questions in one message, three at most. Otherwise ask nothing.

## 2. Rewrite each assumption as a hypothesis

A good hypothesis:

- makes one claim. Split "owners struggle with this and would pay for a fix" into two.
- names who it is about.
- could be false. "People like convenience" can't be wrong, so it can't be tested.
- keeps the student's meaning and adds nothing. Sharpen the wording, don't improve the idea, and don't add facts the student didn't state.

## 3. Tag each one with a type

| Type | What it claims | Where the answer is |
|---|---|---|
| Problem | The problem exists and costs people something | Online |
| People | Who has it, and how many | Online |
| Status quo | What they do about it today, and what they spend on it in money or time | Online |
| Your solution | They would want, use, pay for or switch to this product | Interviews only |

**Anything about the future is "Your solution".** If an assumption says people will or would want, use, pay or switch, it is interview-only, even when it names a kind of product and not the student's own. A search can't answer it, because no page online is about a product that doesn't exist yet.

Keep it on the interview list in the student's own words. Then offer the nearest thing a search can answer, as a separate hypothesis, and say what you changed.

The idea itself nearly always carries one of these that the student didn't list: that people would use the thing at all. Add it to the interview list too, and say it comes from the idea and not from their list, so the interview questions cover it.

> Student: "dog owners will pay a monthly fee for a walking app"
> Interviews only: "Dog owners will pay a monthly fee for a walking app."
> Searchable version (Status quo): "Dog owners pay for dog walking today."
> What changed: "will pay for an app" became "pay for walking today".

**"Nothing good exists" can't be shown by a search**, because you can't search everything. Rewrite it as a claim about what people use today: "Dog owners who use a walking service are unhappy with it today."

## 4. Decide who counts

A page often doesn't show whether its writer is in a narrow group. A review of a walking service may not say whether the writer lives in a city flat. If only words from provably-in-group writers counted, most real accounts would be set aside.

So name the group whose words will count in this run. Use the student's group when a page can show that someone belongs to it. Otherwise use the nearest wider group, such as "dog owners" for "dog owners in city flats". Whether the narrower group differs is a question for interviews, so add it to the interview list.

## 5. Write the test for each searchable hypothesis

Before searching, write down:

- **If true, we'd expect to find:** ...
- **If false, we'd expect to find:** ...

Make both concrete and observable: reviews complaining about a specific thing, people describing a workaround, a survey figure, people paying for a tool.

If the statement has a loose word in it ("often", "a lot", "most", "unhappy"), say in the "if true" line what would count as it. For "most", "often" or "a lot", only a count can settle the question. Single accounts show that it happens, not how often, and the test lines should say so.

The "if false" line is the plain opposite of the statement, not a neighbouring claim. For "dog owners often skip walks because of work", the opposite is "dog owners say work doesn't make them skip walks", not "they skip walks more because of weather". Check that no single finding could fit both lines.

## 6. Pick which ones to run

A light run takes two hypotheses. Pick the two the rest of the idea depends on most. The student can swap them. Don't propose a third unless the student asks for a bigger run.

## 7. Show it and stop

Send this, then wait for the student's reply:

```
Here's what I'll look up. Tell me if I got anything wrong.

**Your idea:** <one neutral sentence, using only what the student said>
**Whose words I'll count:** <the group>

**Assumption 1:** <statement>
If it's true I'd expect to find <...>. If it's not, I'd expect to find <...>.

**Assumption 2:** <statement>
If it's true I'd expect to find <...>. If it's not, I'd expect to find <...>.

**Can't be looked up, so save these for interviews:**
- <statement>, because <reason>

**Could be looked up, but not this time:** <any other assumptions, or leave this line out>
**What I reworded:** <each change, or "nothing">

I look up two at a time to keep this quick, and picked the two the rest of the idea depends on most. Swap them if you'd rather.

Reply "go", or tell me what to change.
```

Keep this message short and in the student's language. The types (Problem, People, Status quo) and the labels H1, H2 and I1 are your working notes. They go in the ledger, not in this message.

The neutral sentence describes the idea without pitch words such as "innovative", "seamless" or "easy".

The reasons on the interview list are about the search, not about people. Use these:

- for something about the future: "your product doesn't exist yet, so no page online is about it"
- for the narrower group: "this search can't tell which writers are <the narrower group>"

Don't predict what customers will do anywhere in this message.

Don't run any search until the student confirms. This is the one point where they steer.

## 8. After "go"

The wording the student agreed to is the wording that gets tested. Copy the idea, the group, the hypotheses with their tests, the interview list and anything not checked this time into the ledger exactly as confirmed, and don't reword them after you have seen search results.

Then read `steps/2-search.md`.
